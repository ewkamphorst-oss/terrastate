The Veteran's Guide

The Field Manual in the game tells you the rules. This guide tells you how experienced players win: how to run a big empire without endless clicking, how the computer nations think, how to break a stalemate and how the end of the season really works.

One game, three worlds. The rules are the same in all three games; only the names change. The prize of the season is the Nexus in Terrastate, Roma in Romaterra and the Capital Sector in Ironfront. Where this guide names a unit or building, the other games have their own name for it.

1. Run a big empire with few clicks

Training capacity is national, so build bases

Your whole country can train a fixed number of troops per turn. A big order does not appear at once: it is split over the coming turns. A Military Base adds the most capacity, a Heavy Complex and a Factory add some too, and the Industrial path and the Supply research raise it further. Want a big army fast? Build capacity first, then order.

Quick recruit

On Army, Quick recruit spends a quarter or half of your treasury in one tap on the units that give you the most attack and defence for your money. Use it when you need troops now and do not want to work out the mix yourself.

Let the chores run by themselves

The game has standing orders for the boring parts: a build plan (up to 5 buildings that start as soon as you can pay), a research plan, Keep my army (it trains back up to the size you choose) and auto-buy on the market for what runs out. Set them once and spend your turns on decisions.

Rally point

On Army › Defend a land, pick one land and press Rally here. Every 3 turns the troops that were trained or came home since then march there by themselves, while a quarter of your army always stays home. Put it on your front line and stop walking troops over by hand.

Bigger is not always better

A huge army fights less efficiently than its size suggests: logistics drag it down, to as little as half its strength. Supply Trucks in the army and the Supply research soften that. A few strong units in a smaller army often hit harder than a crowd of cheap ones.

2. How the computer nations think

No dice

The same battle always ends the same way. There is no luck in combat, so the Calculator shows you the exact result before you send a single soldier. Use it before every big attack.

They only know what their scouts saw

A computer general judges your land by his scouting report. If he has not scouted it, he guesses, and his guess can be wrong. He calls off an attack he thinks will fail, so a well defended land is often left alone. But do not count on the exact minimum: his guess changes, and a scouted weak spot gets hit.

Watch for the feint. A nation that is set on you and has scouted a weak land may first send a small army at another land of yours, to pull your defence away, and strike the weak land two turns later. A small attack coming in? Check your other lands before you move everything.

A long memory

Computer nations remember who attacked them and who helped them, and they band together against whoever runs too far ahead. A non-aggression pact with your neighbours early on keeps you out of a war on two fronts later.

Allies, distance and time

Computer allies send troops when you are attacked, but only help that arrives before the attack lands counts. Help from your own sector is quick; from another sector it takes about five turns, and from the other side of the world much longer. Radar warns you earlier, which gives your allies more time. Help that arrives after the battle stays to guard the land against the next wave.

Deals

On Army › Defend a land, next to each nation that is coming at you: Buy peace (it leaves your lands alone for 20 turns and turns back the armies already on the way) or pay a neighbour to hit them (that nation marches on your attacker as soon as it can win; if it cannot within 6 turns, you get half your money back).

3. Breaking a stalemate

Around turn 100 the borders are often full of bunkers and forts, and a head-on attack costs far too much. Open the attack window on a target that is too strong: it lists the ways round the wall. In short:

Spies first

Spy missions knock out a target's radar or power grid for 3 turns, raid its treasury, stir up unrest, steal research points or put its best commander out of action for 6 turns. Hit the radar or the commander just before your army lands.

Heavier troops

The top units come from the doctrines in Research. The Juggernaut doctrine brings huge, heavy units (Titan Mechs in Terrastate) that pack a lot of power into a small army; the Asymmetric doctrine brings fast swarms and elite recon (Swarm Drones in Terrastate). One army of these breaks a wall that ten ordinary armies bounce off.

Together

A joint attack with your alliance lands as one blow. Combined, the alliance can take what no member can take alone.

The dirty trick

Break a non-aggression pact and strike at once: you get a one-time surprise bonus of +35%. The world will remember it.

4. The endgame: the prize of the season

To win the season you take the prize (the Nexus, Roma or the Capital Sector), build its command centre and hold it for a number of turns. Two rules decide most endgames.

At most 3 armies fight there at once

Only three attacking armies fight at the prize at the same time. Every other army waits outside and shows waits on the map; it goes in as soon as a place is free. Ten small armies therefore block the door for your strongest one.

Attacker: send a few very strong armies, not a swarm of small ones. Quality wins here, not numbers.

Momentum for the holder

Once the command centre stands, the holder's defence grows by 15% for every turn it is held, up to +150% after 10 turns. Losing a single battle there does not reset the count; only losing the prize itself does.

Defender: only three enemy armies can hurt you per turn while your defence keeps growing. Keep troops flowing in (a rally point on the prize does that), give your alliance a Rally Call, and ride it out.
